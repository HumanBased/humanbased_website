What you do with your life is best decided by combining three things that writing on meaning keeps returning to: becoming deeply good at something, contributing to something larger than yourself, and knowing yourself well enough that your choice fits your values, strengths and natural pull. None of the three gives you a finished plan, but together they give you a direction you can start walking in this month.

If you are asking this question, you probably feel the weight of it. It can seem as though everyone else was handed an answer at some point and you missed the day. Almost nobody got a handed answer. Most people who seem settled built it slowly, and the pieces they used are simpler than the question makes them look.

## Begin with knowing yourself

The third piece on the list is where the work starts, so take it first. A choice about your life only fits if it is made by someone who knows what they value, what they do well, and what they are drawn to when nobody is steering them. Without that, you end up choosing from what is on offer: the job that was open, the path your family understood, the thing that looked impressive from outside.

Knowing yourself is practical rather than mystical. It means being able to say, in plain sentences, which kinds of work leave you with energy, which kinds drain you, what people come to you for, and what you would refuse to give up even under pressure. Most people have never written any of that down. Doing so often brings a small shock of recognition, because the answers were there all along and nobody had asked.

## Get good at something, deeply

The first of the three is mastery: becoming so skilled at something that when you do it, time seems to disappear. Psychologists call that state flow, and the researcher who gave it its name, Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, spent his career asking when people feel most alive. In *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience* he wrote that the best moments in life do not come from relaxing or drifting.

> “The best moments usually occur when a person’s body or mind is stretched to its limits…”
>
> Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience*

He went on to describe a voluntary effort toward something difficult and worthwhile. The point holds for most of us: absorption comes from effort that is demanding but within reach, repeated until you are good at it.

This is useful when you are lost, because it turns an enormous question into a smaller one. You do not need to know what to do with your whole life to start getting good at one thing. Pick something that already holds your attention a little: a craft, a language, a way of solving problems, caring for people, building things. Give it regular, focused time for a year. Mastery is slow and mostly unglamorous, and it has a way of showing you what you actually enjoy, which no amount of imagining can do.

Choosing what to master is where self-knowledge comes back in. Years of practice on something that does not suit your temperament will feel like grinding. Years on something that does will feel like becoming yourself.

## Add your part to something larger

The second piece is contribution, helping to build toward a better society. It is easy to underrate because it can sound grand. In practice it is quite ordinary, and it helps to look at how much of your own life rests on it.

Many of us live more comfortably than people did in the past. We walk on streets someone laid, drink from systems someone designed, live in cities that earlier generations planned and paid for, and rely on knowledge and institutions that people spent their lives building without ever seeing the full result. Each generation inherited a great deal and passed on something a little better. Adding your part to that chain is meaningful in a way that few other things are, because it connects your effort to people you will never meet.

Your part does not have to be public or large. A teacher, a nurse, a plumber who does honest work, a parent raising someone decent, a developer who builds something people can trust, a neighbour who keeps a street alive: each is laying a stone. Viktor Frankl wrote in *Man’s Search for Meaning* that life keeps setting tasks for each of us, and that finding meaning means taking responsibility for fulfilling them. His words were:

> “…the tasks which it constantly sets for each individual.”
>
> Viktor Frankl, *Man’s Search for Meaning*

In the same book he named doing a deed as one of the ways meaning can be found, and he wrote of responsibility toward other people and toward an unfinished work. A fair reading of all this is that meaning often arrives when you stop asking what life can give you and start asking what you can give back.

## Putting the three together

Each piece alone can mislead. Mastery with no contribution can become a lonely perfectionism. Contribution with no self-knowledge can turn into burnout in a role you were never suited to. Self-knowledge with no action stays a private hobby. When all three are present, something clicks: you are doing work that suits you, that you are getting better at, and that matters to someone else.

A simple way to test any option you are considering is to ask three questions of it. Does it fit what I value and what I am naturally drawn to? Could I get properly good at it, to the point of losing track of time? Does someone benefit from it, now or later? An option that scores well on all three deserves a serious trial. An option that scores on none is probably a distraction, however attractive it looks.

## What to do this week

Do not try to decide your whole life. Choose a direction for the next six months and treat it as an experiment. Write down what you already know about yourself, even if it fits on half a page. Pick one skill to practise on a schedule. Find one small way to be useful to other people that uses it, such as helping a friend, volunteering for a few hours, or sharing what you are learning.

At the end of the six months, ask what gave you energy, what dragged, and what you would do more of. Adjust and go again. Over a few rounds of that, a direction forms that you could not have reasoned your way to in advance. The question “what do I do with my life?” gets answered by living a good draft and revising it.

## Key takeaways

- Three things combine to answer the question: mastery, contribution and self-knowledge.
- Start with knowing yourself, so the choice fits your values, strengths and what you are drawn to.
- Mastery means getting so good at something that time seems to disappear while you do it. Csikszentmihalyi called this flow.
- Contribution means adding your part to what earlier generations built and future ones will rely on. It can be small and ordinary.
- You do not have to decide everything. A six-month experiment beats waiting for certainty.
