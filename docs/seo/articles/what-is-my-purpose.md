# What is my purpose?

- Meta title: What Is My Purpose? A Plain Answer | PurposeBased
- Meta description: Your purpose is what feels meaningful to you, fits your values and draws on what you are naturally pulled toward. You build it by practising it, and it starts with knowing yourself.
- URL: https://www.humanbased.org/purposebased/articles/what-is-my-purpose
- Published: 2026-10-12

Your purpose is the thing you give your time and effort to that feels meaningful to you, fits your values, and draws on what you are naturally pulled toward and perhaps good at. Nobody hands it to you whole: you develop it, practise it and make it yours, and it can change as you do.

That is the short answer. The longer one matters because most people ask this question on a quiet evening when something feels slightly off, and a single sentence does not help much with that. So here is the longer version, written plainly.

## Purpose is something you build

A lot of us carry a picture of purpose as a hidden answer, a single correct job or mission sitting somewhere out there while we wander around failing to find it. It is an understandable picture, and it makes the search feel like a test you are failing.

A more useful picture is closer to a skill. You do not discover that you are a good cook by thinking hard about it. You cook, you notice what works, you cook again, and slowly it becomes yours. Purpose behaves in a similar way. You try something, you pay attention to how it feels and what it asks of you, and you keep going or you adjust. Over months and years the thing you practise becomes something you can say you stand for.

Viktor Frankl, the psychiatrist who wrote about finding meaning even in the worst conditions, put the personal nature of this clearly in *Man’s Search for Meaning*.

> “For the meaning of life differs from man to man, from day to day and from hour to hour. What matters, therefore, is not the meaning of life in general but rather the specific meaning of a person’s life at a given moment.”
>
> Viktor Frankl, *Man’s Search for Meaning*

Notice the words “a given moment”. The answer Frankl points to is never reached once and filed away. You meet it again and again, in the circumstances you are actually in.

## What a purpose is usually made of

People describe their purpose in very different words, but when you look closely the same few ingredients keep turning up.

First, it is meaningful to you. Not impressive to others, not sensible on paper, meaningful to you. You can tell the difference because meaningful work leaves you feeling that your time went somewhere, even when the day was hard.

Second, it lines up with your values. If honesty matters to you and your work asks you to bend the truth, no title or salary will make it feel like purpose. If you value care for others, a role with no contact with people may leave you restless however well it goes. Values are the ground the rest stands on, and they are easy to forget until something grinds against them.

Third, it draws on something you are naturally drawn to. There are subjects you keep returning to without being asked, problems you find interesting when other people find them dull. That pull is information. It is worth following for a while to see where it leads.

Fourth, and often as a result of the third, it may involve something you are good at. Talent is not required for purpose, and plenty of people find meaning in things they will never be the best at. But many people find that what they are drawn to and what they do well overlap more than they expected, and that the overlap is where effort starts to feel like energy.

## It can change, and that is fine

The purpose that fits you at twenty-five may not fit at forty-five. A person who finds meaning in building something from nothing may, later in life, find it in teaching what they learned. Becoming a parent, losing someone, moving country or simply growing older all shift what matters. If your purpose shifts, you have not necessarily got it wrong. Often it means you were paying attention.

There is one catch. You cannot know whether something is your purpose until you have applied yourself to it properly. Half an effort tells you very little. A month of trying a new craft and quitting at the first dull patch does not show that the craft is wrong for you, only that the first dull patch arrived. The only way to learn whether a direction fits is to give it enough of yourself that it can push back, reward you or disappoint you honestly.

Joseph Campbell, who spent his life studying the stories people tell about meaning, said it this way to Bill Moyers in the conversations collected as *The Power of Myth*.

> “If you follow your bliss, you put yourself on a kind of track that has been there all the while, waiting for you.”
>
> Joseph Campbell, *The Power of Myth, with Bill Moyers*

“Bliss” can sound grander than most of us feel on an ordinary Tuesday. It helps to read it as whatever leaves you most alive and most yourself, and to treat following it as a matter of small, repeated steps.

## How do you discover it? Start by knowing yourself

The difficulty is simple. To find out what fits you, you have to know who you are, and most of us have never been asked to look at that carefully. School tells us what we are good at in a narrow range of subjects. Work tells us what we are paid for. Hardly anything asks what we value when no one is grading, or what we would do with a free afternoon and no obligations.

So the first piece of work is about you, before it is about any job or mission. Some questions that tend to open things up:

- When did you last lose track of time, and what were you doing?
- What do other people come to you for, without you ever advertising it?
- What would you refuse to do, even if it paid well and nobody would find out?
- Which activities leave you with more energy than you started with, and which leave you flat?
- What would you regret never having tried?

You do not need to answer them well. Write the first honest thing that comes. If you would like a structured place to do this, we made a free [self-discovery journal](https://www.humanbased.org/purposebased/self-discovery-journal) with prompts on exactly these themes. It runs in your browser and nothing you write is sent anywhere.

Mary Oliver ended her poem “The Summer Day” with a question that does a lot of this work on its own.

> “Tell me, what is it you plan to do / with your one wild and precious life?”
>
> Mary Oliver, *“The Summer Day”*

Nobody can answer that with a career plan alone. It asks you to notice that you are choosing, every day, whether or not you admit it.

Another line is often credited to the theologian Howard Thurman, though it reaches us through a friend’s recollection of a conversation rather than from his own writing. The advice runs roughly like this: instead of asking what the world needs, ask what makes you come alive, because the world needs people who have come alive. Whoever first said it, it points in a useful direction. Purpose that comes from aliveness lasts longer than purpose assembled from what you think you should want.

## Then practise it

Once you have a few honest observations about yourself, turn them into small experiments. If you noticed that you light up when you explain things, offer to teach one short session, write one page, or mentor one person for a month. If you noticed that you hate the open-plan noise and love long uninterrupted stretches, look at how you could rearrange a week to protect one. Keep each experiment small enough that failing costs you little, and long enough that you learn something real.

After each one, ask three plain questions. Did it give me energy or take it? Did it sit well with my values? Did someone benefit? The answers will not always be tidy. Over time they form a pattern, and that pattern is much more trustworthy than a sudden conviction in the middle of the night.

Expect to hold your answer loosely. You will make a provisional commitment, live inside it, and revise. That is what developing a purpose looks like from the inside, and it is a much calmer process than the hunt for a single correct answer.

## Key takeaways

- Purpose is built and practised over time. It is not a hidden answer you either find or miss.
- It usually combines what is meaningful to you, what fits your values, and what you are naturally drawn to and may be good at.
- It can change as you change. Revising it is part of the process.
- You only learn whether a direction fits by applying yourself to it properly.
- The way in is knowing yourself: your values, strengths and what gives you energy.
